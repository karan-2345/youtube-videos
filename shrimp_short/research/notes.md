# Research notes: the pistol shrimp's snap

Checked on 2026-11-28 with web search. The cloud session can't open YouTube or most publishers' full texts, so the
facts come from abstracts, the authors' own lab page and press coverage quoted in search results. Re-read the key
abstracts on the laptop before publishing.

## Facts used in the script

| # | Claim in the script | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | It knocks out fish with a claw that never touches them | Versluis et al. 2000, *Science*; University of Twente lab page: the pulse from the collapsing bubble is "so strong that little fish or shrimp get stunned or even killed by it and are then eaten" | The claw shuts in front of the prey; the bubble does the work. |
| 2 | The claw snaps so fast that its water jet tears open a bubble | Versluis et al. 2000: the snapper claw shoots a high-velocity jet (about 25 m/s from high-speed video); its pressure drops below the vapour pressure of water and a cavitation bubble forms | The script says "tears open a bubble", not "makes air": it's a vapour bubble. |
| 3 | The bubble collapses with a crack that can stun or even kill a small fish | Versluis et al. 2000: hydrophone and high-speed video show the sound comes at the bubble's collapse, not when the claw shuts. Source levels up to about 190-210 dB (peak to peak, re 1 µPa at 1 m) | The script avoids "louder than a gunshot": underwater and in-air decibels aren't comparable. |
| 4 | As it collapses it flashes with light, so for an instant it's nearly as hot as the Sun's surface | Lohse, Schmitz & Versluis 2001, *Nature* 413, 477 ("Snapping shrimp make flashing bubbles"): a short flash at collapse ("shrimpoluminescence") implies at least 5,000 K inside the bubble. The Sun's surface is about 5,800 K | FLAG 1: the temperature is inferred from the light, not measured, and the flash is far too faint to see by eye. "Which means" keeps the direction right (light, so heat). |
| 5 | When thousands snap at once the crackle can drown out navy sonar, which happened in World War Two | Scientific American and WHOI: in WWII the crackle of shrimp beds interfered with the US Navy's submarine sonar, and the University of California Division of War Research traced it to snapping shrimp | FLAG 2: the claim that US submarines *hid* in the noise rests on a 1940s Time piece and a children's article, so the script doesn't make it. |

## FLAGs

1. **The Sun comparison is an estimate.** At least 5,000 K, inferred from the flash; the script says "nearly as hot",
   and the picture labels it ESTIMATED. The real flash is invisible to the eye, so the picture shows it as a
   slowed-down, brightened close-up.
2. **Sonar, not hiding.** Interference with WWII sonar is well documented; submarines hiding in the noise isn't, so
   the script leaves that out.
3. **Already covered?** Web searches found no Zack D. Films pistol-shrimp video, but that check isn't conclusive (the
   same kind of search missed their goosebumps videos), and vidIQ was out of credits. Many other channels have made
   pistol-shrimp Shorts, so ours leans on the cut-away of the claw and the sonar ending. Zack D. Films has made
   onion, goosebumps and quicksand videos, so those topics were dropped.
4. **Not researched for the script:** claw switching (if the snapper is lost, the small claw grows into a new one) is
   well supported (Read & Govind 1997) and could replace beat S04 if the sonar ending doesn't work.

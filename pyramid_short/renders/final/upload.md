## Description (YouTube)

The Egyptians dragged stones heavier than a car across the desert, and the clue to how was hiding in plain sight:
a tomb painting from about 1850 BC shows about 170 men pulling a giant statue on a sled while one man pours water
on the sand in front of it. In 2014, physicists at the University of Amsterdam tested it with a small sled on a tray
of desert sand: with just the right amount of water (about 2 to 5 % of the sand), the force needed to pull it
dropped by about half, because tiny water bridges between the grains make the sand about twice as stiff. Too much
water turns it to mud and the sled sinks again.

What's simplified: the painting (in the tomb of Djehutihotep at Deir el-Bersha) is about 700 years younger than the
Great Pyramid and shows a statue, not a pyramid block, so it doesn't prove the pyramid builders used the trick, only
that Egyptians knew it. How the pyramids were built is still debated: the water trick is one piece, and other
finds point to ramps, canals and boats. The painting is redrawn here; the original scene was destroyed in 1890 and
is known from a photograph taken the year before.

Sources:
- A. Fall et al., "Sliding Friction on Wet and Dry Sand", Physical Review Letters 112, 175502 (2014)
